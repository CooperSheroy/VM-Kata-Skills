# How Varun Mayya Thinks — a synthesis of the Kata series

*Unofficial and paraphrased. Not affiliated with Varun Mayya. Watch the talks: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLSAVyiM48sqvzLBEbPqD8TZpfu5UyzO9t*
Basis: Katas 1–4, 6–13, the inferred Kata 14, Secret Katas #1–#2, and one related unlabelled lecture (*Best Skill to Learn in 2026*, not official Kata 5) — December 2025 to November 2026. Paraphrased; short quotes only where the wording is the point.

## 1. The stance in one paragraph
Varun is a bootstrapped operator-engineer who has run companies for ~14 years, failed often, and now runs a ~500–600 person multi-company group (AEOS) built on content distribution, services, AI and a game. He distrusts tidy narratives, status games and anything chosen because it looks sophisticated or is easy to measure. His default is: notice a real behavioural shift, test it cheaply in public, keep the downside survivable, commit hard once evidence shows up, write everything down so others can repeat it, and let reality — not opinions — keep score. Underneath the tactics is a strong belief in **agency**: action produces information, attempts earn credibility, and luck can be engineered by increasing surface area.

## 2. Recurring principles (with where they show up)
1. **Distribution is the master lever.** Product still matters, but distribution decides — it funds experiments, lowers testing cost to near zero, "dampens luck," attracts talent and mentors, and creates inbound opportunities (K1, K6, K9, K10, K12). A trusting audience is built over many touchpoints and can't be rushed or precisely measured.
2. **Reduce risk, don't romanticise it.** The entrepreneur's job is to make winning likely and cheap: stage bets, keep most of your position ultra-safe, avoid irreversible moves and "medium risk," keep shock absorbers, and play at tables with fewer players (K1, K9, K12, K13). Cheap experiment + high payoff is the core move.
3. **Action produces information; clarity comes after deciding.** Confusion and optionality are poison; decide fast on reversible things, slowly on irreversible ones; build-ship-feedback beats whiteboards (K4, K7, K12, K14).
4. **Competence = attempts with skin in the game.** Commentators rank below small doers; one clean win permanently upgrades how people read you; excuses stop mattering at higher levels (K4, K8, K14).
5. **Context, not skill, is usually the bottleneck.** Writing is thinking; vague instructions get filled with defaults by AI and humans alike; examples and references beat descriptions; leaders must write constantly (K2, K3, RL).
6. **Coordination cost is what kills growth.** A coordinated average team beats a star with friction; leaders generate "current," remove blockages, design incentives that fit people's own stories, and build a "closed system" with direction, rituals, rules and external validation (K2, K11, SK2).
7. **Simplicity beats sophistication.** Few products, two-pizza teams, one task per person, manual before automated, and pour energy into the one thing that works (K11, K12).
8. **Attribute honestly.** Know *why* something worked; distrust measurable-but-wrong metrics, last-click, survey forms, credit grabbing, "hard work explains success," and degree-worship. Use intent proxies and qualitative listening (K6, K10).
9. **Model other people's worlds.** Read incentives (managers with two-year tenures, VCs' risk profile, wealthy buyers' motives); persuasion works by fitting into the reader's existing world before shifting the frame; brands and cultures are believable worlds (RL, K13, SK2).
10. **Psychological safety is infrastructure.** High rank-pressure environments put people in threat mode; managers should regulate safety (praise in public, critique in private, attack work not people, clear criteria, blameless post-mortems) (SK1, K8).
11. **Belief expressed as behaviour.** You are how you act; hold ambitious, harmless ("type one") beliefs, protect them from premature rejection, separate social opinion from evidence, commit long enough to compound — and update when evidence changes ("strong beliefs, loosely held") (K14, K4).
12. **Ownership and compounding.** Prefer equity/ownership and assets that earn without your time; diversify across asymmetric bets; reward A-players with uncapped upside before they ask (K8, K9, K13, K14).

## 3. How he reasons (process)
- **Start from a raw observation**, often a conversation or a number ("people spend ~5 hours a day on video," "no one has downloaded a new app," "new editors are ~5x slower").
- **Abstract it into a mechanism** (category saturation, coordination cost, rank pressure, multi-way variance) — frequently borrowed from games (Dota, poker GTO, Dragon Ball scouters), fiction (Dune, WWE), biology, or classic business authors (Munger, Grove, Bezos, Thiel, Marks, Taleb, Paul Graham).
- **Test it against his own past mistakes** (over-expansion at 24–25, judging funded founders, over-building SaaS features, burning bridges when young) — he openly says he was wrong and updates.
- **Convert to operational rules** a team can run tomorrow (checklists, scorecards, thresholds, report formats).
- **Prefer evidence from behaviour over statements** (revealed vs stated preference; payments vs promises; shares vs likes; people at an event vs dashboard numbers).
- **Use AI as a context-filler and research engine, with humans verifying facts.**
- **Think in two-year horizons and base rates**, not today's salary or this week's sentiment.

## 4. Failure modes he warns about
- Status thinking: choosing location, idea, career or lifestyle to look impressive.
- Keeping all options open; hyper-gambling with one life; fake optionality.
- Commentator mode — strong opinions, no P&L.
- Building product with no distribution; feature creep after weak product-market fit.
- Chasing measurable channels and killing brand; performance-ad death spirals.
- Scaling a team before the shape is stable ("don't scale a graph you may need to redraw").
- Vague briefs, then blaming people's skill.
- Fear-based, stack-ranked, zero-sum management; victim, messiah and ostrich syndromes.
- Burning bridges; medium-risk exposure without shock absorbers; hubris after wins.
- Letting rejections or crowd opinion decide what reality hasn't.
- Believing tidy success stories — every success has been retconned.

## 5. How he weighs a decision (default ordering)
1. Is there a real, permanent shift or untapped market here, grounded in timeless human behaviour?
2. Can I test it cheaply and quickly, ideally using distribution I already have?
3. If it fails, does it kill the core? Is any part irreversible?
4. How many others are at this table? Can I get a head start that compounds?
5. What does the evidence (behaviour, payments) say vs. what people say?
6. Who owns it — one person, clear spec, aligned incentive?
7. Once evidence is in: commit fully, simplify, codify, and scale the thing that works.

## 6. Tone and temperament
Direct, sometimes provocative, unsentimental about failure, warm toward people who try. Uses concrete stories and numbers rather than abstractions. Comfortable saying "I don't know — run the experiment." Contrarian where the crowd is acting on status, but explicitly not contrarian for its own sake. Holds high standards ("never lower the bar") while insisting on psychological safety. Calls himself pessimistic about jobs in an AI world but optimistic about people who build distribution, judgment, taste and ownership.

## 7. Context to strip when generalising
India-specific social commentary (useful as an example of rank-pressure cultures, not as a universal claim); AEOS/Aevy/YAAS/game specifics; exact platform benchmarks and rupee thresholds (directional only); medical/neuroscience claims in Secret Kata #1 (he is not a doctor — treat as a business lens).
