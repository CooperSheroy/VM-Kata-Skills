# Decide and Commit

## Source
Varun Mayya, *Career Advice for People Who Feel Lost | Kata 7* (YouTube, March 2026): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ia_bqazMeIw
A distilled, paraphrased method — not a substitute for watching the talk.

## Overview
Confusion, not lack of ability, is what stalls most people. Clarity comes *after* deciding and acting. Optionality looks safe but dilutes impact. Commit to one thing long enough to work; diversify only without endangering it.

## When to use
- **Career:** feeling lost, choosing between jobs, fields, or further study.
- **Projects:** too many side projects or initiatives running at once.
- **Founders:** pivot paralysis; sampling ideas without committing.
- **Organisations:** a decision nobody wants to own.

## When not to use
Three sibling skills overlap; pick by the core problem:
- **decide-and-commit (this skill):** the person has *several options and hasn't chosen*. Output is a choice and a commitment.
- **belief-to-behavior:** the person *has already chosen an ambitious goal* but isn't acting on it or is losing conviction after rejection.
- **risk-staging-playbook:** the person *has a specific risky move in mind* and asks whether it's too risky or how to stage it.
If a choice involves one irreversible high-stakes option, decide here which option, then run risk-staging-playbook on it.

## Steps
1. **List the options and classify the stance.** How many live options? How long undecided? Thiel quadrant: indefinite optimist ("keep doors open"), definite pessimist (hoarding for worst case), indefinite pessimist (no plan), definite optimist (specific plan). Aim for definite.
2. **Separate confusion from diversification.** For each secondary pursuit: "Is the primary thing already working?" and "Does doing this cost me the primary thing?" If the primary isn't working → it's confusion; cut. If it is and the secondary doesn't threaten it → acceptable diversification.
3. **Diagnose why they're stuck.** Fear of the wrong pick (overweighting downside); status games (collecting impressive options); wanting to look different; advice from people who haven't succeeded; organisations where nobody wants to own the call.
4. **Door test.** Reversible (two-way door)? Decide today. Irreversible (one-way door)? Take weeks, gather real evidence, then decide.
5. **Evidence through action.** Default to build → ship → feedback. Define the smallest real test for the top 1–2 options (a working product, a paying client, a shipped project). Others' results aren't your evidence.
6. **Rule of three.** If sampling, cap at three serious attempts, then commit.
7. **Prefer growth rate.** Between environments, prefer fast-growing ones over large but plateaued ones — adjusted for personal risk capacity.
8. **Commit.** Write: "For the next N months I am doing X. I am stopping Y and Z. I will review on DATE using these signals." Ask for forgiveness, not permission, on reversible moves.

## Output
Options with stance and door type, the confusion-vs-diversification verdict, the smallest test for the top option(s), and the written commitment statement with review date.

## Watch for
- Hyper gambling: many long shots for an individual with one life.
- Freelancer's trap: absorbing payment and client-concentration risk alone.
- Endless "should I do A or B?" without a test.

## Questions to ask
- "If you could only do one thing for a year, which one?"
- "Which option makes the others easier or unnecessary?"
- "Can this be undone? Then why haven't you decided?"
