# Simplicity Filter

## Source
Varun Mayya, *How Simplifying Everything Makes You Rich | KATA 11* (YouTube, July 2026): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0tbeJZYvsg4
A distilled, paraphrased method — not a substitute for watching the talk.

## Overview
Complexity is a cost that compounds. The most valuable work is turning complex things into simple ones others can absorb. Keep products few, teams small, ownership single, and automate only what's been done manually.

## When to use
- **Product:** feature requests, roadmap bloat, too many SKUs or offers.
- **Operations:** a proposed new process, tool, or rule; build-vs-buy; automate-vs-manual.
- **Team:** busy-but-slow teams, people with two jobs, oversized teams.
- **Messaging:** a pitch or offer that takes a paragraph to explain.

## When not to use
- The project is genuinely uncertain and needs a plan for unknown work, team shape, or experiment thresholds → use **resolution-project-manager**. Tie-break: simplicity-filter = *cut and focus an existing plan or proposal*; resolution = *run a new, uncertain project end to end*. A resolution plan can be passed through this filter afterwards.
- The friction is between people (ego, incentives, alignment) → use **coordination-cost-audit**.

## Steps
1. **The simplicity question.** "Does this make the overall system simpler or more complex?" List every downstream thing it forces to change (hiring, training, tools, dependencies, docs, customers). If the list is long, the burden of proof is high.
2. **Focus audit.** How many products/offers/initiatives exist? Could each be explained in one consistent sentence? Which one actually works (revenue, usage, love)? Put most energy into selling and scaling that before building more. Remove or freeze anything that exists mainly to look sophisticated.
3. **Attention reality check ("distracted user model").** Assume the audience (customers or teammates) is half-paying attention on a phone. What 1% will they remember? Simplify the message to that.
4. **Team design.** Teams of ~6–10; split larger ones into autonomous units. One task, one owner; don't top up a good person with a second task. Identify the few core teams that generate capital; protect them from overhead.
5. **Build vs. use.** Can existing tools, vendors, or AI do this? Build only what's truly differentiating.
6. **Manual first.** Before automating or hiring for a role, do it manually enough times to prove the need and learn its shape. Before building a tool, solve the problem by hand for real cases.
7. **Feature discipline.** Once one feature works, stop adding features nobody asked for; shift effort to distribution and sales until the core stops working.

## Output
A simplified version of the plan: what to cut, what to keep, single owners, team shape, what to do manually first, and the one message to communicate.

## Questions to ask
- "What's the simplest version of this that still keeps quality?"
- "If we adopt this, what else has to change?"
- "Who owns this — one name?"
- "Have we done it by hand yet?"
